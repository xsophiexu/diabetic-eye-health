Diabetes & Your Eyes

Diabetes is one of the leading causes of vision loss in working-age adults, and early damage often has no symptoms. The good news: a yearly dilated eye exam and good diabetes care can prevent most severe vision loss.

When should I get my eyes checked?

How diabetes affects the eyes

High blood sugar over time damages the tiny blood vessels in the retina, the light-sensing layer at the back of the eye. Diabetes also raises the risk of other eye diseases.

Diabetic retinopathy

The most common diabetic eye disease. Damaged retinal blood vessels leak or close off. In the non-proliferative stage, vessels weaken and leak. In the advanced proliferative stage, fragile new vessels grow, can bleed into the eye, and can cause scar tissue and retinal detachment.

Diabetic macular edema (DME)

Fluid leaks into the macula, the part of the retina used for sharp central vision such as reading and recognizing faces. DME can happen at any stage of retinopathy and is a major cause of vision loss in people with diabetes.

Cataracts

Clouding of the eye's natural lens. People with diabetes tend to develop cataracts earlier and more often than people without diabetes.

Glaucoma

Damage to the optic nerve, often linked to pressure inside the eye. Diabetes roughly doubles the risk of open-angle glaucoma, and proliferative retinopathy can cause a severe form called neovascular glaucoma.

Warning signs

Early diabetic eye disease usually has no symptoms. Do not wait for changes in your vision to get an exam.

Tell your eye doctor about

  • Blurry or fluctuating vision
  • New spots, strings, or "floaters"
  • Dark, empty, or missing areas in your vision
  • Straight lines that look wavy
  • Colors that seem faded or washed out
  • Trouble seeing at night

Get care the same day if you have

  • Sudden loss of vision in one or both eyes
  • A sudden shower of new floaters or flashes of light
  • A shadow or curtain moving across your vision
  • Eye pain with redness and blurred vision

These can signal bleeding, retinal detachment, or acute glaucoma. Call your eye doctor right away or go to an emergency room.

Protect your sight

  1. Get a dilated eye exam every year, or as often as your eye doctor recommends.
  2. Manage blood sugar. Work with your care team on an A1C target that is right for you.
  3. Control blood pressure and cholesterol, which also damage blood vessels in the eye.
  4. Don't smoke, or get help quitting.
  5. Stay active and eat well to support overall diabetes control.
  6. Take medicines as prescribed and keep up with regular medical visits.
  7. Plan ahead for pregnancy. Retinopathy can worsen during pregnancy, so get an eye exam before or early in pregnancy.

An exam with an ophthalmologist or optometrist uses dilating drops so the doctor can see the retina. Some clinics also offer retinal photography, including FDA-cleared AI screening, in primary care offices.

Treatment options

Treatment works best when started early. It can slow or stop damage and sometimes improve vision.

Anti-VEGF injections

Medicines injected into the eye that reduce swelling and stop abnormal vessel growth. A first-line treatment for DME and used for proliferative retinopathy.

Laser treatment

Focal laser seals leaking vessels. Scatter (panretinal) laser shrinks abnormal new vessels in proliferative retinopathy.

Steroid treatment

Steroid injections or implants in the eye can reduce macular swelling when other options are not enough.

Vitrectomy

Surgery that removes blood or scar tissue from the gel inside the eye in advanced disease.

Already living with vision loss? Low vision rehabilitation can help you keep reading, cooking, getting around, and working with tools such as magnifiers, lighting, and screen readers.
